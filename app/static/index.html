Hedge or warehouse?

A dealer sees pair-trade orders from a population of clients. Every ticket is one decision: hedge it (flat, minus the cost of going to market) or warehouse it (take the other side, and win exactly what the client loses). The baseline desk always hedges. This desk runs two models — one that predicts where the underlying goes, one that predicts whether this client is any good — and warehouses only when both say the client is on the wrong side.

How the decision is made

A ticket is a pair trade collapsed to one spread return. Client c picks side s ∈ {+1,−1} and realises r = s·x, so skill is side-selection: P(s = sign x) = 0.5 + qc and E[r] = 2·qc·E|x| — different mean per client, the same variance unless you switch the σ regime. The client's own side is evidence about x, weighted by how sharp we think they are, so the two models combine by Bayes: P(up | model, s, q̂) ∝ Pmodel(up)·P(s | up, q̂), and the desk warehouses when −E[r] > hedge cost. Set q̂ = 0 and it reduces to the price model alone; set Pmodel = 0.5 and it reduces to the client model alone — which is exactly the attribution shown above.

Synthetic data throughout. No client-spread revenue is modelled: this is the pure risk-warehousing decision, so every number is edge over a desk that runs flat.
